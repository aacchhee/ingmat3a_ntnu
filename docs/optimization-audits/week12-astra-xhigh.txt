## Prioritized corrections

### P1 — Say precisely what small residuals establish

**12.3, lines 306–309.** “Residualene sier kun at den beregnede kandidaten ligger nær det vi forventer” can be read as a distance-to-solution guarantee. Neither equality residual nor stationarity residual supplies that guarantee by itself. The farthest circle point has both residuals exactly zero with its own multiplier.

Replace the sentence with the concrete claim that the constraint and stationarity equations are approximately satisfied; small residuals alone neither classify the point nor bound its distance to a minimum. The immediately preceding Cauchy–Schwarz proof is the global guarantee. The existing deep-reading comparison of the closest and farthest points is correct and supports this distinction.

### P1 — Define constrained stationarity before using its name

**12.1 immediately after lines 102–105; first unexplained use is 12.2 line 173.** Week 8 explicitly defines “stasjonært” by `∇f = 0`. Week 12 uses a different, constrained notion without explicitly naming that change. This is the most important definition-order gap.

After the boxed equations, say that a feasible point is **stasjonært under likhetsbetingelsen** when the first derivative of the objective is zero in every feasible tangent direction; at a regular point this is equivalent to existence of a multiplier satisfying the Lagrange equation. Explicitly say that the ordinary objective gradient need not be zero. The circle example already supplies the necessary contrast `(1,2) ≠ 0`; no new example is needed.

### P2 — Supply two short bridges in the first worked calculation

**12.1, lines 106–111.** The first central Lagrange calculation jumps from the two equations to both exact points and multipliers. Add the short elimination `y = 2x`, followed by `5x² = 1`, before the answer. This is enough for students who use the notes as their only text.

The attribution “Cauchy–Schwarz fra uke 4” is not supported by the current week 4 text: it develops dot products, lengths, and projections, but does not state the named inequality. State the inequality locally in one sentence, such as `|u·v| ≤ ||u||₂ ||v||₂`, and explain that the absolute inner product is at most the product of the lengths, before the displayed application. Week 6 uses the inequality in a deep-reading argument, so a week 6 link is possible, but a short local statement is more useful here. No long proof is required.

### P2 — Make the exercise wording support the intended reasoning

1. **`week12-dual-bound`, lines 558–567.** All three numerical answers are correct: `c = 2`, `min L = 3/4`, and `F(1,0) = 1`. However, comparing a lower bound with one arbitrary feasible value does not determine whether the bound is sharp. Explicitly ask students to compare `3/4` with the exact minimum value from 12.3, `(sqrt(5)-1)²/2 = 3-sqrt(5)`. Since `3/4 < 3-sqrt(5)`, this multiplier's bound is not sharp. Alternatively, have them show the unique unconstrained minimizer of this SPD Lagrangian, `a/2`, is infeasible. The first option is shorter and follows the main example directly.

2. **`week12-python-normal`, lines 579–582.** “Kontrollen undersøker normalens lengde” is false: the tests compare both gradient components in three points. Say that the control checks the components of the normal vector. The title can simply be “normalvektor”; the task does not compute a tangent. There is no need to add more work to the exercise.

### P2 — Tiny terminology corrections while touching these passages

- **12.2 depth, lines 200–203:** replace “tilfredsstillende lokal minimumstest” with “tilstrekkelig test for et strengt lokalt minimum”. State the maximum version with negativity in **all nonzero tangent directions**, mirroring the preceding quantifier. The mathematics intended here is correct.
- **First occurrence of `enhetstangent`, line 205:** write “enhetstangent, altså en tangentvektor med lengde 1”. This is a short composition of known concepts, but defining it prevents a needless new-term gap before exercise 1.
- **12.4 depth, lines 434–437:** replace “den ubetingede minimatoren”/“minimatoren” with “minimumspunktet uten bibetingelser”.

## Mathematical checks

- Linear circle problem: `∇f=(1,2)`, `∇h=2z`, stationary points `±(1,2)/sqrt(5)`, multipliers `∓sqrt(5)/2`. Upper and lower objective values are `±sqrt(5)`.
- Product problem: neither coordinate vanishes in the Lagrange system; `4λ²=1`; the four diagonal points exhaust the candidates. The stated inequalities prove both global extremes. The displayed reduced Hessian gives `-2` at a maximum and `+2` at a minimum.
- Distance problem: `F(z)=0.5||z-a||²`, `∇F=z-a`; the nearest point is `a/sqrt(5)`, objective `3-sqrt(5)≈0.7639320225`, plus-convention multiplier `(sqrt(5)-1)/2≈0.6180339887`. The farthest stationary point has objective `3+sqrt(5)` and multiplier `-(sqrt(5)+1)/2`, confirming why zero residual is not a classification.
- The projected multiplier `-∇F·∇h / ||∇h||²` is correct for a nonzero equality normal. It is intentionally reconstructed rather than obtained from a solver result attribute. This is compatible with the specified SciPy 1.14.1 runtime.
- Degenerate example: `(0,0)` is the only feasible point and hence both a global maximum and minimum; the Lagrangian gradient cannot vanish for any multiplier.
- Dual formula `d(λ)=5/2-λ-5/[2(1+2λ)]` is correct for `1+2λ>0`; at the selected `λ*`, its global minimizer is feasible. No general strong-duality claim for arbitrary nonconvex equality problems is made.
- Exercise answers checked algebraically: `(0,4)` and `(1,0)`; `(1,2)/sqrt(5)` and `-sqrt(5)/2`; `1/2` and `-2`; residuals `0` and `1`; `(0,0)` and zero constraint gradient; `2`, `3/4`, and `1`.
